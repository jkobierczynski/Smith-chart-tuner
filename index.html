What the chart is actually showing

The Smith chart is a polar plot of the complex reflection coefficient Γ = (Z − Z₀) / (Z + Z₀), redrawn with a grid of impedance instead of raw numbers. Every point inside the outer rim is an impedance: the curved grid lines are circles of constant resistance and arcs of constant reactance, so reading a point's position off the grid tells you R and X directly. The rim itself is |Γ| = 1 — total reflection, as seen by a dead short or a wide-open circuit. The exact center is Γ = 0: the load equals Z₀, nothing reflects, and the meter reads a flat 1.0.

The two tuner controls are two different ways of moving across that grid. A series reactance only changes X and leaves R untouched, so it slides your point along one of the cyan constant-resistance circles. A shunt susceptance only changes conductance, so — in the impedance chart shown here — it slides you along one of the amber constant-conductance circles instead. Chain one of each, in either order, and you can reach the center from almost any starting point: that's the whole reason a two-element L-network can match nearly any real-world antenna.

Feedpoint impedances for the presets are simplified, illustrative curves — not measured antenna data.

Field NoteFeedline caveat

The tuner sits at the transmitter end of the cable. Tuning it only forces the impedance at that end back to 50 Ω, which is what the SWR meter is reading. The mismatch — and the standing waves — between the tuner and the antenna itself are completely unchanged; a lossy line still burns extra power in those standing waves no matter how nicely this meter reads. An antenna tuner protects your transmitter, it doesn't repair your antenna.
